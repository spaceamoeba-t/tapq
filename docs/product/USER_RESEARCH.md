# TapQ User Research

**Status:** Working research repository  
**Last updated:** 2026-09-08

## 1. Research objective

Understand when users want AI assistance without looking at or touching a screen, and whether existing earbuds can provide a socially acceptable interaction surface.

---

## 2. Early qualitative observations

### Observation A — Wake phrases can feel socially awkward

In daily scenarios, users may avoid saying an explicit wake phrase in front of coworkers, classmates, or strangers. The problem is especially visible in quiet, professional, or socially sensitive contexts.

**Hypothesis:** silent or low-visibility input such as nod/shake/tap can increase assistant usage in contexts where a spoken wake phrase would feel awkward.

### Observation B — AI power users and AirPods users overlap in the early-adopter cohort

Interviews with colleagues working in technology showed repeated overlap between users of products such as Claude Code, Codex, ChatGPT, and Gemini and users who regularly wear Apple products, especially AirPods.

**Hypothesis:** TapQ can reach an early-adopter segment without requiring a new hardware purchase.

**Research caution:** interviewees skew toward highly educated technology workers. Do not generalize this observation to the full consumer market without broader sampling.

### Observation C — Professionals encounter terminology they do not want to ask about publicly

Feedback from a prior investor and follow-up interviews/survey responses indicated that professionals sometimes hear definitions, abbreviations, company names, or domain terms they do not understand during meetings or conferences.

Users may avoid:

- interrupting the speaker,
- signaling lack of familiarity,
- visibly searching on a phone,
- losing attention while looking something up.

**Hypothesis:** a short private explanation delivered in-ear, gated by a nod, can preserve comprehension and social presence.

### Observation D — Students experience an analogous comprehension gap

In lectures, students may encounter concepts they do not understand quickly enough to ask or research without missing subsequent content.

**Hypothesis:** session capture + context-aware clarification + structured notes can improve perceived comprehension and post-class review.

### Observation E — Translation is valuable but increasingly commoditized

Travelers benefit from hands-free translation, but major ecosystems and specialist hardware already provide real-time translation.

**Hypothesis:** TapQ should add context and action on top of translation rather than compete as a translation engine.

## 3. Current evidence quality

| Insight | Evidence type | Confidence | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wake phrases can be socially awkward | observation + interviews | Medium | frequency not quantified |
| AI power users often already use AirPods in current cohort | qualitative interviews | Medium | sample is tech-heavy and highly educated |
| Business meeting terminology creates private clarification need | investor input + interviews + survey | Medium | sample size and segment distribution not documented |
| Lecture clarification is useful | personal/peer observation | Low-medium | needs dedicated student research |

## 5. Business Mode research plan

### Study A — 15–20 discovery interviews

Target mix:

- product/engineering,
- consulting,
- finance/investing,
- research,
- sales/business development,
- founders/executives.

Core questions:

1. Tell me about the last meeting where you heard something you did not understand.
2. What did you do in the moment?
3. Why did you choose that response?
4. How often does this happen?
5. Would a private in-ear explanation have helped?
6. Would an AI prompt itself be distracting?
7. What meeting content would you never want recorded or sent to a cloud model?
8. How do you currently take notes?
9. What would make you trust or distrust this product?

Avoid leading with the solution until after the current behavior is understood.

### Study B — Meeting diary

Ask users to track 10 meetings and log:

- unfamiliar terms,
- questions they chose not to ask,
- phone/laptop searches,
- missed decisions,
- post-meeting reconstruction work.

This establishes real event frequency instead of relying on memory.

### Study C — Prototype intervention test

Simulate meeting audio and trigger a private “want an explanation?” prompt.

Compare:

- proactive + nod,
- manual voice request,
- manual phone lookup,
- no assistance.

Measure:

- time to understanding,
- perceived interruption,
- social comfort,
- comprehension,
- preference.

## 6. Survey questions for next round

Recommended survey items:

1. How many meetings do you attend in a typical week?
2. How often do you hear an unfamiliar acronym or term in a meeting?
3. What do you usually do when that happens?
4. How comfortable are you asking the speaker to explain?
5. How comfortable are you searching on your phone during the meeting?
6. Would you use private in-ear clarification if it required a nod to activate?
7. How many proactive offers per 30-minute meeting would feel acceptable?
8. Do you regularly wear AirPods or other earbuds during work?
9. Which AI assistants do you use weekly?
10. Would you allow meeting transcription if all participants had consented?
11. Would local/on-device processing materially increase willingness to use it?
12. Which output is most valuable after a meeting: summary, decisions, action items, glossary, transcript, follow-ups?

---

## 7. Research ethics / privacy

- Do not upload raw interview recordings or identifiable transcripts to the public repository.
- Store only aggregated or anonymized findings publicly.
- Obtain consent for research recording.
- Do not treat investor anecdotes or a convenience sample as statistically representative.
- Separate observed behavior from interpretation and product hypothesis.
